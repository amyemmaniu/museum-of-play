Is there a wrong way for children to play?

Painter and sculptor Camila Lucia Muñoz believes that not only is play inherently political, but ascribing to rigid notions of correct and incorrect ways to play stifles creativity, growth, and autonomy.

## Description

In *Protocols of Play*, Muñoz turns a critical eye to Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA), a widely used therapy in which play is often treated as a skill to be taught, measured, and rewarded. In many ABA programs, a child's play is broken into goals: stacking the blocks, taking turns, making eye contact, using the toy "as intended." Behavior that falls outside those goals, such as lining up cars, spinning a wheel, or replaying the same scene again and again, may be redirected rather than understood.

Muñoz, who grew up alongside autistic family members and now works with neurodivergent young people, asks what is lost when play becomes a protocol. Drawing on autistic self-advocates' accounts and her own conversations with families, she reimagines the language of therapy sessions (data sheets, token charts, and prompts) as paintings, sculptures, and interactive installations.

Her work asks who decides what counts as "appropriate" play, and whose comfort that definition protects. When one way of playing is treated as the standard, every other way becomes a deficit, and the line between teaching and policing quietly blurs. Muñoz traces how those ideas about "normal" play carry into schools, playgrounds, and public policy, and how they become political.

## In the gallery

- **The Correct Table.** A sculptural play table set with toys and a laminated list of approved uses. Visitors are invited to follow the instructions, then ignore them, and to notice how each choice feels.
- **Token Economy.** A wall of hand-painted tokens and reward charts, rendered in bright enamel. The tokens have been rearranged so that no chart can be completed.
- **Rewrite the Rules.** A participatory wall where visitors can propose new rules for play and pin them up next to the old ones. Muñoz will add selected contributions to the exhibition throughout its run.

## How to visit

- Plan to spend about 30 minutes in the exhibition.
- Visitors are encouraged to touch, stack, spin, and rearrange the works in *The Correct Table* and *Rewrite the Rules*. There is no wrong way to participate.
- The exhibition addresses therapy and childhood, and it may bring up personal experiences. A quiet seating area is available just outside the gallery.
- The installation has been designated as a Sensory-Friendly Space, meaning its light and sound levels are suitable for those with [sensory needs](/plan-your-visit)
